#### Core Development & Rationale
The 2025 New York City Energy Conservation Code (2025 NYCECC) became fully enforceable on March 30, 2026 — and it introduces a set of changes that fundamentally alter how NYC building enclosures must be designed, tested, and documented during construction. Published by Simpson Gumpertz & Heger (SGH), one of the leading building enclosure engineering firms in the country, this analysis details the specific compliance demands that now apply to every NYC project filed after enforcement activation: a mandatory air tightness threshold of 0.40 CFM/ft² at 75 Pascals, proven through staged blower door testing at multiple construction milestones (not just at project close-out), expanded thermal bridging disclosure requirements in construction documents, and the elimination of blanket historic building exemptions in favor of registered design professional justification reports.

#### Key Industry Impacts
* **Real Estate**: Developers should anticipate non-trivial schedule and budget impacts: staged blower door testing requires contractor coordination to stage interior air barrier completion before wall assemblies are closed in, which conflicts with standard fast-track construction sequencing; failing a mid-construction test now requires opening completed assemblies, a cost exposure that should be carried as a contingency line item on every NYC project budget.
* **Engineering & Construction**: MEP and enclosure engineers must now coordinate air barrier continuity through penetrations (MEP sleeves, structural connections, window perimeters) from design development onward — not as a close-out activity — and thermal bridging at structural connections, balcony slabs, and facade attachments must be explicitly calculated and documented in construction documents, raising the technical bar for building envelope detailing across all project types.

#### Strategic Takeaway
Any NYC project team that has not specifically briefed the general contractor on staged 2025 NYCECC blower door testing protocols is already behind — the code is live and failing a mid-construction test now carries direct schedule and financial consequences.
